TEXT: I don't think I disagree with that, but:

For 99.99% of programmers, learning about compilers and interpreters never resulted into "I'm building compilers & interpreters at work", but more "I now know how parsers work and this stuff works, so I can debug this or build a little thing that parses something, or ..."

Now, with these models getting so much better, it's very easy to whip up a parser or debug things. Less internal knowledge required. Sure, if you work on the Go team at Google, and you're responsible for the GC, you need to know your stuff.

But consider this: whether a project uses Ruby/Python/JavaScript/TypeScript in Aug 2026 is a completely different choice for me than it was in Aug 2024. Completely different! Now it's mostly the runtime that I worry about. The syntax? The docs? The primitives? The libraries? Far less important.

So, the usefulness of that knowledge has changed and the relative importance of languages has changed.

That being said: I do still think it's important to know how stuff works. Absolutely.

But if someone were to offer me a "JIT Compiler Engineer" role somewhere, would I take it? That was a dream job of mine years ago. Wouldn't do it now.

The bigger levers for programmer productivity are no longer language & frameworks.
